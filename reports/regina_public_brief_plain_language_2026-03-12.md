# Regina Lead Service Connections: What Changed, What Hasn’t, and What to Watch Next

Date: 2026-03-12  
Audience: Public-facing (plain language)

## Bottom line
Regina did not stand still on lead service connections. Council made a sequence of formal decisions from 2021 to 2025 that expanded replacement rules, financing access, and filter support. That is real policy movement.  

But the movement is mostly **incremental** (program design and implementation changes), not a full strategy reset.

## What the record clearly shows

1. **2021: Major baseline decision**  
   Council approved a 15-year replacement program (target year 2036), required private-side replacement when City-side replacement occurs, and added support measures.

2. **2022: Implementation mechanics**  
   Executive Committee moved bylaw-change recommendations to implement enhancements tied to the 2021 decision.

3. **2023: Ongoing oversight direction**  
   Council directed continued reporting on program updates.

4. **2025: Another meaningful adjustment package**  
   Council approved expanded financing access, removed one admin fee, increased annual filter reimbursement, added a one-time filter installation reimbursement, and directed additional outreach/reporting actions.

## Why this matters
This pattern is **formal continuity**: decisions were not isolated one-offs. They were extended through later committee/council actions, with added administrative tools over time.

This also reflects **practical pressure**: repeated delegations and filed public communications appear in official records at key decision points.

It also matters for a second reason: this is not only a governance or infrastructure story. The project’s research base already supports a broader health framing in which lead exposure is linked with child cognitive harm, ADHD-type burden, and later chronic disease risk. In public terms, that means delay is not only about dollars spent or pipes left in the ground — it is also about quality of life, development, and healthy years put at risk.

## What has not yet been proven in this brief
- This brief does **not** claim that public delegations directly caused each vote outcome.
- This brief does **not** claim that policy changes are already sufficient to eliminate exposure risk.
- This brief does **not** claim a complete textual reconstruction of the older MN19-23 chain beyond what is explicitly captured in currently assembled primary records.

## What would count as a true policy shift (not just incremental change)
A stronger shift would include most of the following, in adopted and funded form:

1. A materially faster completion timeline than 2036, with budget authority.  
2. Clear equity-targeted acceleration rules for high-risk households/areas.  
3. Legally anchored service standards for outreach, filter delivery, and follow-up.  
4. Annual public dashboard reporting with neighborhood-level progress and financing uptake.  
5. Measurable reduction targets tied to replacement completion and exposure mitigation.

## Public accountability checklist (simple version)
- Are annual reports actually tabled and public?  
- Are reimbursement and financing tools being used by households most at risk?  
- Are renters and landlord-mediated properties being reached in practice?  
- Are Council-directed follow-up reports (e.g., Q4 2026 items) delivered on time?

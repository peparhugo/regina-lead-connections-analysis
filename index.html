CASE FILE: REGINA LEAD SERVICE CONNECTIONS

Compiled April 2026 from public records

1,801 μg/L.

That was the lead reading in one North Central home. 360 times Health Canada's safety limit. We downloaded the city's own data — five public databases, 99,119 records — and traced a chain of deferral from City Hall to the provincial cabinet to Ottawa. At every level, someone had the power to act. This is what we found.

01 The water

Cathedral Village is where the city's lead problem stops being abstract.

In September 2025, 54 homes were tested. Seventy-one percent exceeded Health Canada's 5 μg/L safety limit. Several results were above 100 μg/L. One measured 570 μg/L — 114 times the standard.

In North Central, a home tested at 626 μg/L in 2023 and was still on a city-owned lead service connection. Another reached 1,801 μg/L before the connection was replaced.

Context

In Flint, Michigan, one household at 104 μg/L triggered a state of emergency and National Guard deployment. In Regina, 20 homes have exceeded that level since 2017.

Brita-certified filters are rated for a maximum of 150 μg/L. Some Regina homes are above the capacity of the protection they are being given. Only 27% of affected addresses had filter coverage in 2024. Only 2% of lead service connection homes are tested each year.

Source: Patricia Elliott, Get The Lead Out, Executive Committee, Sept. 17, 2023

If the water can be this high and the sampling this thin, what exactly decided it would stay in the ground?

02 The vote

The city did not stumble into a slower schedule. It recorded one, publicly, and chose it.

On May 26, 2021, council had a faster option in front of it: finish all replacements by 2031. The amendment failed 4–7. The city chose 2036 instead.

Kathleen Donovan of the Cathedral Area Community Association and Steve Wolfson of Regina addressed council that day. Then the 15-year option passed 11–0.

But the city was not the only institution in the room. The Saskatchewan Health Authority's senior public health inspector, George Koutsoulis, was listed in attendance. The health system that will pay $32 million in treatment costs watched the slower timeline get adopted — and left no visible mark in the decision record.

The deeper question is not just why council chose slow. It is why the system makes slow the default. Lead replacement in Regina is not a standalone capital project. It is dispersed across utility budgets, reserve transfers, and road-repair work — with no dedicated budget line. The city funded a $51.3 million aquatics facility and $29 million for wastewater. Those projects are named, financed, and politically visible. Lead is fragmented and easy to defer.

What did this decision cost — and who keeps paying while the system processes it?

03 The cost

The delay has a price. The fix is cheaper than inaction.

The math that ends the debate
$34.4M to replace every remaining lead connection — city and private side combined
$40.9M the economic burden if they stay in the ground

Every dollar spent on replacement avoids $1.19 in downstream burden. Newark replaced 18,000 lines in 24–30 months at no cost to residents. Saskatoon is nearly done. Regina has replaced about a third.

The burden falls on 481 children currently living in homes with lead service connections. Each child faces an estimated $84,950 in lifetime economic cost — ADHD treatment, learning support, reduced earnings, and adult chronic disease. Families absorb 60%.

144 quality-adjusted life years IQ loss is permanent. Each child carries it for 30 years.
$40.9M total economic burden Families 60%. Healthcare 17%. Schools 12%.

The five-year delay alone — the difference between 2031 and 2036 — adds 107 more children through the critical development window and $4.1 million in burden. North Central bears $825,000 of it.

And the harm doesn't hold still while the city processes it. Every year the lines stay in the ground, new children are born into lead-connected homes and enter the exposure window. Older children age out, but the system keeps replenishing the harmed population. Since 2019, an estimated 997 unique children have already been exposed. If nothing changes, 802 more will be by 2036. The policy timeline never catches up to the harm timeline.

The city's completed replacements have already prevented $32.5 million in burden and spared an estimated 637 future children from exposure. The program works. The investment has a positive return. So why hasn't the province — or the federal government — forced it to go faster?

04 The hardest hit

The burden does not spread evenly. It clusters. And the policy ignores it.

Saskatchewan has no provincial lead-in-water standard. No mandatory landlord disclosure — annual city letters go to property owners, not tenants. In North Central, 60% of housing is rental. Tenants drink the risk without knowing it exists.

The replacement order makes this worse. In Regina, lead pipes are replaced when road repairs are already scheduled — not when health risk is highest. That means neighbourhoods with less street reconstruction, often the poorest and most neglected, wait longest. The city outsourced lead risk management to the people with the least power to manage it: renters who cannot order replacement, cannot compel disclosure, and may not even know the pipe is there.

In September 2025, council adopted CR25-115 — expanded payment access, fee removal, filter reimbursement, and annual reporting. That is progress. But it is a shift from replacing pipes faster to making slow replacement slightly easier. The question is no longer whether the city has a program. It is whether the program can reach the people most likely to be missed: renters, Indigenous families, low-income households in the neighbourhoods where road repairs haven't been scheduled.

The city blames the province. The province says it's municipal. The health authority says it's not their file. That is not a misunderstanding. It is a system designed to move responsibility sideways. The child at the end of the pipe is exposed because every institution assumed someone else would fix it faster.

Burden model (v5 — 2021 Census + comorbidity correction)

Population: 1 connection = 1 household. Children per household from 2021 Census. 481 children on lead.

Healthcare: Bottom-up: ADHD 12% ($8K/yr, 12yr), LD 20% ($10K/yr, 8yr), Behavioral 10% ($4K/yr, 8yr), CVD 7% ($5K/yr, 15yr), CKD 5% ($15K/yr, 10yr). ×0.85 comorbidity. $36,950/child.

Earnings: 2 IQ pts × $24,000/pt (Salkever, Regina median). $48,000/child.

QALY: 0.015/yr × 30yr NPV = 0.30/child. Between GBD borderline ID (0.011) and mild anxiety (0.030).

IQ validation: Cathedral testing confirms 71% exceed 5 μg/L. Dose-response: 0.7–2.8 IQ points at measured levels.

Convergence: Top-down Gould and bottom-up condition costing agree within 3% on earnings.

Error model & data forensics

Coverage: 84.1% ± 2.9% of completed replacements appear in GIS. Calibrated against city-published counts 2020–2023. The undercount is systematic — same rate every year.

Five systems: CBMH (6,943), Trace (5,405), LeadPolyB (2,760), Lead Areas (3,637), GTLO (~2,305). No official reconciliation exists.

Entry lag: Median 90 days. Batch entries: 2,021 records on Jan. 23, 2024.

Finance: 26 documents, 2019–2025. Aggregate utility spend proved. Project-level lead spend proof is missing.

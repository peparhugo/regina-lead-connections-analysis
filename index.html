CASE FILE: REGINA LEAD SERVICE CONNECTIONS

Compiled April 2026 from public records

1,801 μg/L.

That was the lead reading in one North Central home. 360 times Health Canada's safety limit. The connection was eventually replaced. But across Regina, 976 children still drink water through lead service connections. We downloaded the city's own data — five public databases, 99,119 records — to find out how this happened, who decided, and what it costs.

01 The water

Cathedral Village is where the city's lead problem stops being abstract.

In September 2025, 54 homes were tested. Seventy-one percent exceeded Health Canada's 5 μg/L safety limit. Several results were above 100 μg/L. One home measured 570 μg/L — 114 times the standard.

In North Central, a home tested at 626 μg/L in 2023 and was still on a city-owned lead service connection. Another reached 1,801 μg/L before the connection was replaced.

Context

In Flint, Michigan, one household at 104 μg/L triggered a state of emergency and National Guard deployment. In Regina, 20 homes have exceeded that level since 2017.

Brita-certified filters are rated for a maximum of 150 μg/L. Some Regina homes are above the capacity of the protection they are being given.

Source: Patricia Elliott, Get The Lead Out, Executive Committee presentation, Sept. 17, 2023

2% of lead service connection homes are tested each year

If the water can be this high and the sampling this thin, what exactly decided it would stay in the ground?

02 The vote

The city did not stumble into a slower schedule. It recorded one, publicly, and chose it.

On May 26, 2021, council had a faster option in front of it: finish all replacements by 2031. The amendment failed 4–7. The city chose 2036 instead — five more years of children drinking through lead pipes that could have been replaced.

Two members of the public addressed council that day: Kathleen Donovan of the Cathedral Area Community Association and Steve Wolfson of Regina. Then the main motion — the 15-year option — passed 11–0 unanimously, including the four who had wanted faster.

The record is not ambiguous. The city rejected the faster schedule and adopted the longer one. What did those extra five years cost?

03 The cost

The delay has a price. It is not rhetorical. It is modeled.

The difference between completing by 2031 and 2036:

772 connections still active in the 2031–2036 window
107 additional children through the critical 0–4 development window
$4.1M additional economic burden. North Central bears $825,000 of it alone.

But the delay cost is only part of the picture. The full burden of lead exposure across Regina — for the 976 children currently living in homes with lead service connections — is measured in two tracks:

293 quality-adjusted life years IQ loss is permanent. Each child carries it for 30 years.
$77.5M total economic burden Families absorb 60% through their children's reduced lifetime earnings.

The math that ends the debate
$34.4M to replace every remaining lead connection in Regina — city and private side combined
$77.5M the economic burden if those connections stay in the ground

Every dollar spent on replacement avoids $2.25 in downstream burden. The fix costs less than half the cost of inaction. Newark replaced 18,000 lines in 24–30 months at no cost to residents. Saskatoon is nearly done. Regina has replaced about a third in the same time Flint replaced all 11,000.

City-side cost: $36M for 3,600 connections ($10,000/connection, CR21-90 report). Private-side: $50–70M for 7,000–8,000 connections. Inflation-adjusted to 2026: ~$22,000–$23,000 combined per connection. Sources: CTV 2021, NRDC 2024

The city's completed replacements have already prevented an estimated $37.4 million in burden and preserved 143 QALYs of children's health. The program works. The investment has a 2:1 return. The question is not whether to fix it — it is why the city chose to take five more years.

04 The hardest hit

The burden map does not spread evenly. It clusters.

The highest burdens concentrate where renter shares, low-income shares, and Indigenous populations are higher. But the replacement rate still sits flat at 35–40% across vulnerability terciles. There is no equity-weighted prioritization in place. If the map is this clear, why does the replacement order still ignore it?

05 The ask

A parent does not need a citywide average. A parent needs to know whether their water is safe now.

Start with the address, not the assumption. If you do not know whether your home has a lead service connection, call Service Regina at 306-777-7000 and ask for the status of your address, whether it is in the replacement program, and whether you should be using a filter and getting a test.

If you rent, call the Office of Residential Tenancies at 1-888-215-2222 or ORT@gov.sk.ca and ask what disclosure and protection apply to your unit.

If you need the city to answer for the timeline, call the City Clerk at 306-777-7262 or the Mayor's Office at 306-777-7339, mayor@regina.ca.

If you need provincial follow-up, call the Ministry of Health at 306-787-7345, he.minister@gov.sk.ca, or the Water Security Agency at 1-866-727-5420.

The policy ask

For council
Accelerate the removal timeline. The 15-year plan chose 2036 over 2031. The cost of that choice is modeled and public. Revisit it.
Adopt equity-weighted replacement scheduling. North Central, Cathedral, and Heritage should be first, not equal.
Expand testing and triage by severity. Testing 2% of homes per year is not a surveillance system. Homes with known exceedances should drive removal priority.
Require landlord disclosure. Tenants cannot protect themselves from a hazard they do not know about.
For residents
Check your address on the city's lead service connection page.
Request a free filter if your home is affected.
Request water testing to know your actual level.

If City Hall needs to explain why 2036 beat 2031, the public record is waiting at the City Clerk's office: 306-777-7262.

Burden model (v5)

Population: 1 lead connection = 1 household address. Children per household = CT children (2021 Census) ÷ CT dwellings. 976 children on lead connections.

Healthcare (bottom-up): Condition-specific prevalence × annual treatment cost × duration, with ×0.85 comorbidity correction for ADHD/LD overlap. $31,365 per child.

Earnings: 2 IQ points per child × $24,000 per IQ point (Salkever model, Regina median income). $48,000 per child.

QALY: 0.015 disability weight/year × 30 years (NPV at 3%) = 0.30 QALY per child. Between GBD borderline intellectual disability (0.011) and mild anxiety (0.030).

IQ validation: Cathedral Village testing confirms 71% of homes exceed 5 μg/L. Dose-response chain (Lanphear 2005) gives 0.7–2.8 IQ points at measured water lead levels. 2.0 is mid-range.

Convergence: Top-down Gould model ($48K/child earnings) and bottom-up condition costing ($31K/child healthcare + $48K earnings = $79K total) agree within 3% on the earnings component.

Error model (84.1% ± 2.9%)
